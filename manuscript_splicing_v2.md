# Abstract

The fall of splicing-factor transcripts with age is among the most reproduced observations in ageing transcriptomics, and most of the evidence comes from cultured fibroblasts. Because these genes are transcribed in proportion to growth, we asked how much of the age signal in these cells reports how fast they divide. Where the division rate of every culture was measured by cell counting, expression of 177 pre-mRNA processing genes follows that rate across 328 libraries (ρ = 0.71). In 107 adult fibroblast donors, proliferation accounts for 70% of the age effect; an attenuation of that size is ordinary among expression-matched random gene sets, but none of 1,000 reaches the size of the association. A decline of about 0.04 standard deviations per decade remains after adjustment, but it is not robust, halving when a second gene-expression programme enters the model; and in a second collection an adjusted decline appears only as a suppression effect. We therefore report no age effect independent of proliferation. Across 63 contrasts from 23 studies the change in splicing genes tracks the cell-cycle change (ρ = 0.86), and eight of twenty-three Reactome ageing programmes follow measured division rate at |ρ| > 0.5. In a preregistered GTEx analysis the coupling is strong in cultured fibroblasts (partial ρ = 0.74) and weak at both skin sites (0.23 and 0.06), where the same proliferation score still recovers the replication programmes. In cultured fibroblasts, splicing-factor abundance is a statement about proliferative state until shown otherwise outside culture.

# Introduction

Few observations in ageing transcriptomics are as consistent as the fall in splicing-factor expression. *SRSF1*, *SRSF2*, *SRSF3*, *HNRNPA1*, *HNRNPD* and their partners are reported at lower levels in cells from older donors, in late-passage cultures and in senescent cells, across laboratories and platforms^1–3^, and about a third of splicing factors change with age in blood from two population cohorts^4^. The observation motivates a broader claim, recently formalised, that loss of splicing homeostasis is a hallmark of ageing^5^, and it has made splicing factors attractive targets: small molecules and pathway inhibitors are selected for their ability to raise splicing-factor expression in senescent fibroblasts, and the resulting loss of senescence markers is read as rejuvenation^6,7^. Under growth-permissive conditions those cells re-enter the cell cycle and resume proliferating^6^.

The strongest evidence for age-associated splicing dysfunction comes from a different measurement. Analysis of mouse and human tissues shows that the fidelity of splicing deteriorates with age — the proportion of isoforms carrying premature stop codons, frameshifts or disrupted protein domains rises, is reduced by calorie restriction and rapamycin, and is linked to age-related changes in particular splicing factors^8^. That work measures the product of splicing. The large literature that measures the abundance of the machinery instead does so mostly in cultured cells, and treats the two as interchangeable.

Whether they can be is a question about cell biology rather than about statistics. Spliceosome assembly, capping and 3′-end processing are required in proportion to the nascent transcription a cell performs; the genes encoding them are transcribed in step with the cell cycle^9^ and are direct targets of the growth programme, MYC activating the core small nuclear ribonucleoprotein assembly genes as it drives proliferation^10,11^. Fibroblasts in culture divide more slowly with each passage and eventually stop; cells from older donors divide more slowly than cells from younger ones; and reprogramming media, conditioned media and most small molecules change how fast cells divide. That splicing-factor transcription follows division rate is therefore expected, and the fall in spliceosomal transcripts that precedes replicative senescence has itself been read as a cause of senescence^12^. What has not been measured is how much of the age signal in cultured fibroblasts division rate accounts for, where the coupling stops, and what survives it.

Testing this requires knowing how fast the cells were actually dividing, not inferring it from the transcriptome. The Cellular Lifespan Study provides that: fibroblasts from healthy donors and from patients with a mitochondrial mutation were grown to replicative exhaustion under nine treatments and two oxygen tensions, with cells counted at every passage, so that a measured division rate accompanies each transcriptome^13,14^. The analysis below uses that measurement as the anchor, asks how much of the donor-age association in a second cohort it accounts for gene by gene, and then asks whether the same coupling holds across publicly available perturbations of fibroblasts. Finally, a preregistered comparison (Supplementary Note 1) of cultured fibroblasts and skin from the GTEx donor pool asks whether splicing-factor abundance is a proliferation readout in tissue as it is in culture.

# Results

## Splicing machinery transcripts fall with donor age and with time in culture

Three independent series of cultured human dermal fibroblasts were compared for genes changing consistently with age (Fig. 1a): two donor-age cohorts and one comparison of late with early passages. A third donor cohort was set aside because its processing batches coincide almost exactly with age strata (Methods). Genes falling in all three series were enriched above all for two programmes: the mitotic cell cycle (283 of 470 genes, BH-FDR 3 × 10⁻⁶⁶) and capped intron-containing pre-mRNA processing (177 of 280, BH-FDR 8 × 10⁻⁴⁵), the latter including 124 of 195 genes of the major splicing pathway and 101 of 149 of 3′-end processing (Fig. 1a). That the cell cycle leads is the first indication of what follows. The 177 pre-mRNA processing genes are used below as the set representing the splicing machinery.

The donor cohort used throughout is not the deposited sample set. Of the 143 libraries in the largest donor series^15^, ten come from patients with Hutchinson–Gilford progeria, a disease caused by activation of a cryptic splice donor in *LMNA*^16^, and 26 more come from donors under 20 years of age; both were removed, leaving 107 normal donors aged 20 to 96 (Methods). Donors aged 83 and over are all from one cell repository, so every model below also carries the repository, the sequencing instrument and sex as covariates, and the results are reported for three further cohort definitions alongside the primary one (Supplementary Table 1). This cohort was defined after the GTEx outcomes were known; the change governs every donor-level analysis in this paper, not only Fig. 6c, and it removed a previously positive result on splicing outcome (Supplementary Note 1).

In those 107 donors the expression of the 177 genes falls with age (*r* = −0.50, 95% CI −0.63 to −0.34, *P* = 4 × 10⁻⁸; Fig. 1b). In four healthy donor lines cultured under identical conditions and sampled for up to 200 days^13,14^, it falls steadily in three (ρ = −0.89, *P* = 1 × 10⁻⁸; ρ = −0.83, *P* = 6 × 10⁻¹⁰; ρ = −0.96, exact *P* = 0.003) and does not fall in the fourth (ρ = +0.77, exact *P* = 0.10), which was followed only to day 55 (Fig. 1c). Because two of the three series that define the set also supply these sample-level data, panels b and c describe rather than independently test the set's behaviour with age.

## Expression follows the measured rate of cell division

The expectation set out in the Introduction — that these transcripts are made in step with growth — and the lead of the cell cycle in Fig. 1a can be tested where division rate was measured independently of the transcriptome; because one of the three series that define the set is this resource's passage comparison, the test is in part a test of the selection (below). The Cellular Lifespan Study allows it: every culture was counted at passage, giving a division rate in divisions per day that owes nothing to the transcriptome. Splicing-factor expression follows it closely across 328 libraries spanning nine treatments, two oxygen tensions and a mitochondrial mutation (ρ = 0.71, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.76, *P* = 1 × 10⁻⁵⁰; Fig. 2a). Those libraries are repeated measures of seven cell lines. Within lines the coupling holds (ρ = 0.62 to 0.88 in the four healthy lines and 0.32 to 0.76 in the three mutant lines; six of seven *P* < 0.05; Fig. 2d), and a mixed model with the cell line as a random effect gives a standardised slope of 0.69 (*P* = 3 × 10⁻⁴⁶; Fig. 2a), 0.71 with a random slope as well, and 0.54 when days in culture is added. Taking one value per line, treatment and oxygen tension — 41 series rather than 328 libraries — the relationship is still there (ρ = 0.54, *P* = 2 × 10⁻⁴). The 20-marker transcriptional proliferation score that stands in for counting in every other figure tracks the counted rate less closely than the splicing set itself does (ρ = 0.63 against 0.71; Fig. 2e), and the dexamethasone arms sit above its fit: their transcriptional score reads as faster division than the cells achieve.

Two selection effects touch this figure and neither accounts for it. The 177-gene set was defined in part on the late-versus-early passage comparison of this same resource, so the correlation in Fig. 2a is not an independent test of it; scored in one matrix the selected set reaches ρ = 0.68, while the unselected Reactome definitions of mRNA splicing and of capped intron-containing pre-mRNA processing reach 0.63 and 0.65 (Supplementary Results 6). The panel of named factors below was itself defined on late-versus-early passage in fibroblasts, so its members were chosen partly for the behaviour shown here. The coupling is a property of the individual factors. Of the twenty splicing regulatory factors assayed in senescing primary human dermal fibroblasts by Holly et al.^4^ — a panel fixed by that publication, in the same cell type, and reported there as age-associated — the median correlation with measured division rate is 0.56, and the most strongly coupled are *HNRNPD* (ρ = 0.79), *HNRNPA1* (0.72), *TRA2B* (0.69), *HNRNPK* (0.66), *SRSF6* (0.65), *SRSF2* (0.65) and *HNRNPM* (0.64) (Fig. 2b). The four factors reported as age-associated in more than one of the studies cited here^2,4^ — *SRSF1*, *SRSF2*, *SRSF6* and *HNRNPK* — lie between 0.58 and 0.66. The three factors that Holly et al. report as higher rather than lower in late-passage fibroblasts do not form a coupled group (*SF3B1* 0.55, *HNRNPUL2* 0.32, *PNISR* −0.06), and the factors furthest from the coupling are those they report as changing least. In untreated cells followed across the culture lifespan, expression and division rate decline together (Fig. 2c).

Division rate was measured in that resource, not manipulated by us, but nine of its treatment arms manipulate it in the same cell lines. Compared with control cultures of the same line and oxygen tension at the nearest time in culture (224 matched cultures; post hoc; Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Results 1), the change a treatment makes to the splicing score follows the change it makes to the counted division rate (ρ = 0.49) and follows the change in the transcriptional proliferation score more closely (ρ = 0.79). Contact inhibition, the largest manipulation of rate available, lowers the rate by 0.35 divisions per day and the score by 0.73 standard deviations. Where a treatment separates the two measures of proliferation, the splicing score usually goes with the transcriptional one: with the counted rate held constant five of the nine arms keep a residual effect at FDR < 0.05 (dexamethasone +0.53, oligomycin +0.56), whereas with the proliferation score held constant seven of the nine keep none, although that score is itself strongly correlated with the splicing score and the adjustment cannot separate them. Two results are explained by neither measure: oligomycin (+0.56 with the counted rate and +0.28 with the proliferation score held constant) and growth at 3% oxygen, which leaves the splicing score 0.21 lower with the counted rate and 0.33 lower with the proliferation score held constant. 2-Deoxyglucose departs in the other direction: it raises the proliferation score without raising the splicing score (−0.32 with the proliferation score held constant) and leaves no residual effect with the counted rate held constant (+0.10, 95% CI −0.04 to 0.24). The claim made here is therefore about proliferative state, not about the counted rate alone.

## Proliferation accounts for most of the donor-age association

If the coupling in Fig. 2 is what produces the age effect in Fig. 1b — the cells of older donors dividing more slowly — then a proliferation measure in the age model should remove most of that effect. The donor cohort has no cell counts, so the 20-marker transcriptional score, compared with counting in Fig. 2e (ρ = 0.63, against 0.71 for the splicing set itself), stands in for the rate here and in every later figure. In the 107 adult donors, splicing-factor expression is nearly collinear with that score (*r* = 0.89, 95% CI 0.84 to 0.92; Fig. 3a). Its age effect is −0.133 per decade (*P* = 6 × 10⁻⁷) and becomes −0.040 (*P* = 0.005) once proliferation is included: 70% of the effect is removed, and what remains is a decline of about four hundredths of a standard deviation per decade. The share removed is 63 to 73% across the four cohort definitions (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Table 1) and 78% when sequencing depth is the only covariate (Supplementary Table 3). The residue depends on the cohort's technical covariates: with sequencing depth as the only covariate the adjusted estimate is −0.022 per decade (*P* = 0.11), and it is the repository term that separates it from zero (−0.038, *P* = 0.004, with depth and repository as the only covariates; Supplementary Table 3); the attenuation itself is similar either way (78% against 70%). The association survives the addition of library-quality measures to the model (−0.110 per decade, *P* = 7 × 10⁻⁶, and −0.039 after adjustment for proliferation, *P* = 0.003; Supplementary Table 3). Because the score tracks the counted rate less closely than the splicing set does (Fig. 2e), adjustment for it is incomplete: the share of the age effect removed is likely an underestimate, and a covariate measured with error leaves a residual even when no proliferation-independent effect exists, so the residue is not by itself evidence of one.

Attenuation of that size is not by itself remarkable. Applying the same adjustment to 1,000 expression-matched random gene sets containing no splicing or cell-cycle genes, 205 had an age association of their own — for most of them an increase with age, not a decline — and the adjustment removed a median of 56% of it; an attenuation as large as 70% was obtained for 27% of them (Fig. 3c). What no random set reproduced was the size of the association on either side of the adjustment: none of the 1,000 reached the unadjusted effect (*P* ≤ 6 × 10⁻⁷), the adjusted effect (*P* ≤ 0.005) or the effect size (largest random |β| = 0.030 per decade). Because the 177-gene set was selected in this cohort for declining with age, the fair comparison for effect size is the unselected Reactome definition, −0.094 per decade rather than −0.133; it too is more than three times the largest random set, and the adjusted residue is not inflated by the selection at all (−0.040 selected, −0.042 and −0.037 for the two unselected definitions; Supplementary Table 5). What distinguishes the splicing machinery is how large its association is, not how much of it the adjustment removes.

At the level of individual genes, 137 of the 177 show an age association, every one of them a decline, and 19 keep a significant decline once proliferation is included; none of the eleven named factors that belong to the set is among them. The share of its age effect that a gene keeps falls as its coupling to proliferation rises (ρ = −0.45 across the 177 genes), which is consistent with a shared dependence on proliferation but is expected in part from the adjustment itself (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Results 2). The genes that keep a decline are not a stable set: 40 in the deposited 142-sample set, 56 when only progeria donors are removed, 19 in the primary cohort and none among adults aged 20 to 82, so that no gene is common to all four definitions and ten are common to the three that keep any (Fig. 3d). The adjustment acts alike on the 127 genes of Reactome mRNA splicing and on the 50 genes of capping, 3′-end processing and export (66% and 78% removed; Supplementary Table 3). In the primary specification a decline remains after adjustment at the level of the whole set, but which genes carry it depends on how the cohort is drawn, and no subset of these genes is recommended here as an alternative index of ageing.

Two further analyses of the same donors are reported in full in the Supplementary Information. An index of splicing outcome computed from the same libraries, the share of junction-spanning reads that fall on unannotated junctions^17^, has no association with age in the adult cohort (β = +1.5 × 10⁻⁵ per decade, 95% CI −3.0 × 10⁻⁵ to +6.0 × 10⁻⁵, n = 107 donors). The association obtained when the 142 deposited samples are analysed together is traceable to progeria donors, children and an oldest stratum drawn from a single cell repository, and the index tracks library quality, so this cohort cannot test whether splicing outcome deteriorates with age (Supplementary Fig. S3, Supplementary Results 3); the tissue-level evidence for age-associated splicing degeneration^8^ stands on its own measurements, not on these. A transcriptome-wide age predictor trained in the same donors (post hoc) reaches *r* = 0.72, its output tracks the proliferation score at *r* = −0.74, and the 20-gene proliferation score alone predicts age at *r* = 0.46; removing the cohort's technical covariates alone lowers accuracy to *r* = 0.24, so the predictor's accuracy cannot be attributed to proliferation alone (Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Results 4).

## The same coupling holds across 63 contrasts from 23 studies

The two cohorts so far vary proliferation through age and passage. If splicing-factor abundance reports proliferative state, whatever moves it, then any perturbation that changes how fast fibroblasts divide should move the splicing set in step, whatever its nature. To test this, 63 contrasts in human fibroblasts from 23 public GEO series were scored for their effect on the 177 splicing genes and, separately, on 533 cell-cycle genes sharing 23 members with them (Supplementary Table 4).

The two track one another closely (ρ = 0.86, 95% CI 0.78 to 0.92, *R*² = 0.92; Fig. 4a), and removing the 23 shared genes leaves this unchanged (ρ = 0.85, *R*² = 0.91). The coupling is specific to the measurement rather than a property of any gene set: in 1,000 random gene sets matched to the splicing set for expression level in fibroblasts and excluding splicing and cell-cycle genes, the largest correlation obtained was 0.56 against the observed 0.86 (empirical *P* < 0.001; Fig. 4d). It is not specific to splicing, however. Scored the same way, Reactome Metabolism of RNA reaches ρ = 0.76 and translation 0.57, while extracellular-matrix organisation runs the other way (−0.60): what follows the cell cycle is the machinery of gene expression as a whole.

Senescence contrasts lie at the negative extreme and the relationship does not depend on them. Fourteen of the 63 contrasts come from the three series that defined the splicing set; dropping all three leaves the relationship unchanged (ρ = 0.84, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.91, *R*² = 0.93, n = 49 contrasts from 20 series). Nor does it depend on the senescence contrasts (without the nine, ρ = 0.79, *R*² = 0.66, n = 54 contrasts; and averaging to one value per study, with each secretome preparation counted separately, gives ρ = 0.79, *R*² = 0.94, n = 29). Because one series supplies twelve of the contrasts, the study-level estimate is the one to read; the contrast-level interval treats contrasts from the same series as independent and is correspondingly narrow. The relationship holds within classes examined separately (Fig. 4b), and after accounting for it no class of perturbation with at least three contrasts — senescence, ultraviolet injury, photoprotection, reprogramming media, culture and metabolic perturbation, or secretome — shows a detectable residual effect on splicing-factor expression (Fig. 4c; raw *P* = 0.22 to 0.85, all BH-corrected *P* = 0.85). With two donor-age contrasts the class is too small to test. The classes without a residual include the interventions intended to rejuvenate fibroblasts^18^, most of which raise the cell-cycle score in these data (15 of 21 contrasts). In post hoc class tests, predicted from fits that exclude the class being predicted, fourteen secretome preparations have a mean residual of −0.02 (95% CI −0.07 to +0.02 across contrasts, −0.12 to +0.05 across the eight series). The seven reprogramming-media contrasts^19^ come from two series and five share a single reference arm, so they are not seven observations: at the series level the class mean is −0.03 with an interval from −1.65 to +1.59, and no class-level statement about reprogramming media can be made from these data. Individual conditions are reported instead, and individual reprogramming conditions depart from the line in both directions (naive medium +0.34, OSK induction −0.18; Supplementary Fig. S4, Supplementary Results 5).

Whether splicing can be told apart from the rest of that machinery was examined post hoc, by comparing the splicing pathways with translation, Metabolism of RNA and rRNA processing after removing the genes they share (Supplementary Fig. S5, Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Results 6). Against the counted division rate the splicing pathways keep their coupling when a comparator is held constant and the comparators keep none, for unselected Reactome definitions as well as for the 177-gene set, which was itself selected in these data; in GTEx cultures, where proliferation is a transcriptional score, the asymmetry is not reproduced and translation lies closer to that score than splicing does.

## Eight of twenty-three ageing programmes report division rate

Fig. 4 already shows that the coupling is not confined to splicing: Metabolism of RNA and translation follow the cell cycle across the same contrasts. If the machinery of gene expression as a whole is transcribed in step with growth, other programmes read as hallmarks of ageing in cultured fibroblasts may be proliferation readouts too, and the counted resource can rank them. Twenty-three Reactome programmes standing for recognised hallmarks of ageing^20,21^, together with the mitotic cell cycle as a positive control, were scored in the 328 counted libraries and correlated with the measured division rate (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 6).

Eight of the twenty-three exceed |ρ| = 0.5, and they are not peripheral ones: chromatin organisation (ρ = 0.69), cellular senescence (0.67), pre-mRNA processing (0.65), mRNA splicing (0.63), interferon signalling (0.60), DNA double-strand break repair (0.59), telomere maintenance (0.58) and base excision repair (0.56). Four of the eight reach or exceed the cell-cycle gene set itself (0.62) and the rest sit just below it. The coupling is not carried by cell-cycle genes sitting inside these sets: removing every Reactome cell-cycle gene from each programme leaves pre-mRNA processing at 0.67 and mRNA splicing at 0.64, with chromatin organisation (0.63) and double-strand break repair (0.55) still above 0.5, while cellular senescence falls to 0.38, interferon signalling to 0.49 and base excision repair to 0.35 (Fig. 5a, right column). Telomere maintenance cannot be assessed this way because all 82 of its measured genes belong to the cell-cycle union. Splicing sits in the middle of this group rather than apart from it, and it is one of the programmes whose coupling is not explained by shared genes.

An equally clear set does not behave this way. Collagen formation is indifferent to division rate (ρ = −0.02), as are the unfolded protein response (0.05), mTORC1 signalling (0.11) and macroautophagy (0.12), while extracellular-matrix organisation and insulin/IGF signalling move in the opposite direction (−0.33 and −0.27). Scored against donor age in the 107-donor cohort, the eight division-coupled programmes all fall with age (ρ = −0.30 to −0.51, FDR < 0.005; Fig. 5b), and after the proliferation score is included only two remain at FDR < 0.05 after adjustment: pre-mRNA processing and mRNA splicing (FDR = 0.04 each), the same specification-dependent residue described for Fig. 3b. That these two pass the threshold is not by itself evidence that splicing is special: after the same adjustment rRNA processing, translation and chromatin organisation keep declines of −0.038, −0.032 and −0.015 per decade (nominal *P* = 0.04 to 0.05) and miss it only after correction (FDR = 0.25); the adjusted estimates for all programmes are in Supplementary Table 6. Collagen formation, which gives one of the strongest raw age correlations, does not survive the cohort's technical covariates (ρ = −0.44 but FDR = 0.25), and the unfolded protein response is marginal before adjustment for proliferation (FDR = 0.07; 0.35 after it); neither is treated here as an age-associated programme, and neither age association reproduced in the GTEx cultures (collagen formation +0.017 per decade, FDR = 0.33; unfolded protein response −0.007, *P* = 0.54; Supplementary Data 2). Splicing and collagen formation, set side by side against division rate and against donor age, show the two behaviours directly (Fig. 5e).

The senescence gene set is worth isolating, because it is the one most often used as a readout of ageing in cultured cells and it moves in the direction opposite to the one expected: its score is higher in faster-dividing cultures (ρ = +0.67). Of its 150 measured genes, 82 also belong to Reactome cell-cycle pathways and those genes carry most of the signal (median ρ = +0.26 versus +0.07 for the remainder, Wilcoxon *P* = 0.003; Fig. 5c); with them removed the set still scores +0.38. The genes a biologist would name as senescence effectors behave correctly and are outvoted: *CDKN1A* (−0.39), *CDKN2A* (−0.31), *CDKN2B* (−0.35), *ATM* (−0.40), *TINF2* (−0.40) and *MDM2* (−0.51) are all higher in slowly dividing cultures, and *LMNB1* (+0.66) and *E2F1* (+0.50), both lost in senescence, are higher in fast ones (Fig. 5d). SenMayo^22^, of whose 88 measured genes one belongs to a cell-cycle pathway, does not follow division rate (ρ = −0.10, *P* = 0.07). A post hoc comparison of seven panels (Supplementary Fig. S6, Supplementary Table 7, Supplementary Results 7) finds that panels with more cell-cycle genes tend to follow division rate more closely (ρ = 0.71 across the seven, exact *P* = 0.09), with one exception, and that none of the five panels with an age association in the donor cohort keeps it once proliferation is included.

We also preregistered the expectation that methylation clocks^23^ computed in the same cultures would follow the same variable (Supplementary Note 2). The sanity check was met (DNAm age rose with cumulative population doublings, ρ = 0.36, n = 86 libraries) and the three predictions were not: no clock reached the preregistered effect size against the counted division rate (Horvath ρ = −0.01, 95% CI −0.10 to 0.08; the skin-and-blood clock and PhenoAge, named conditionally in the preregistration, +0.18 and +0.06; Hannum's clock, added afterwards, +0.14; all below 0.3; n = 471 libraries), the proliferation score was related to DNAm age at ρ = 0.16 (95% CI −0.03 to 0.34, n = 109 paired cultures), which does not exclude the preregistered effect size of 0.3, and the association between the splicing score and DNAm age was not attenuated by the preregistered 50% when the counted division rate was held constant but changed sign (from ρ = +0.15 to −0.34, n = 109 paired cultures; recorded and not interpreted; Supplementary Fig. S7, Supplementary Table 8). We therefore make no statement about epigenetic clocks; the claim here concerns transcriptional readouts.

## In skin from the same donor pool the coupling weakens

Every result so far comes from cultured fibroblasts, in which how fast the cells divide is the main variable that differs between samples and, as Fig. 5 shows, the programmes of replication and of gene expression rise and fall with it together. Bulk tissue largely lacks that variable: most cells are not cycling, and proliferation varies little between donors. If splicing-factor abundance reports proliferative state, then between donors in bulk tissue, where proliferation varies little and most cells are not cycling, the coupling should weaken; that expectation was fixed in advance as H2 for sun-exposed skin and H3 for skeletal muscle (Supplementary Note 1). Two further expectations are our own reasoning and were not preregistered as tests: the programmes that are part of replication itself should still be recovered wherever cycling cells are present, and collagen formation, whose indifference to proliferation in culture is H5, should stay indifferent in skin. Whether splicing-factor abundance is a proliferation readout in tissue as it is in culture was therefore tested in GTEx, where cultured fibroblasts, skin from two sites and skeletal muscle come from one donor pool and were processed by one pipeline^17,24^. Hypotheses, definitions and success criteria were fixed in a preregistration locked before the data were downloaded (Methods, Supplementary Note 1), and all six outcomes are reported (Supplementary Table 9). The comparison of every programme across tissues was preregistered as an exploratory analysis without a success criterion (S1 in Supplementary Note 1); its use as an internal control for the score is ours.

In cultured fibroblasts from 492 donors the preregistered 96-gene version of the splicing set tracks the proliferation score as closely as in the cohorts above (partial ρ = 0.74, 95% CI 0.70 to 0.78, *P* = 1 × 10⁻⁸⁵; Fig. 6a), and collagen formation is again indifferent to it (ρ = 0.05). In sun-exposed lower-leg skin from 739 donors the coupling falls to 0.23 (0.16 to 0.29; preregistered one-sided Fisher *z* = −12.0, *P* = 3 × 10⁻³³), in non-exposed suprapubic skin from 626 donors to 0.06 (−0.02 to 0.13; Fig. 6a), and collagen formation is only weakly related to the proliferation score at both skin sites (ρ = 0.03 and 0.09; not preregistered). Fig. 6b and Supplementary Table 10 give the partial correlation of every programme in every tissue. Thirty-two of the cultures (6%), identified post hoc, lie off the main trend at low proliferation; they are enriched in particular RNA-extraction and sequencing batches and have lower RNA integrity, and excluding them raises the raw correlation from 0.72 to 0.89. They are kept in every estimate reported here, as the preregistration specified. The fall in skin is not a failure of the score to register proliferation. In the same donors, with the same covariates and the same proliferation score, the programmes tied to DNA replication are still recovered — the mitotic cell cycle at 0.67 in sun-exposed and 0.60 in non-exposed skin, double-strand break repair at 0.64 and 0.58, base excision repair at 0.62 and 0.58, telomere maintenance at 0.61 and 0.52 — while pre-mRNA processing, mRNA splicing, translation and the senescence set lose theirs. Those programmes are expressed in the cycling cells that the score indexes, so their recovery shows that between-donor differences in the cycling fraction are resolved in this tissue; it does not show that a between-donor test in bulk tissue is as sensitive for a programme expressed in every cell, and the tissue result is read as a statement about what the measurement reports (Discussion). Skeletal muscle from 839 donors, the third preregistered tissue, gave ρ = −0.12 (−0.18 to −0.05), which meets the locked criterion for H3 (ρ < 0.3) and is recorded as supported in Supplementary Table 9. After the outcomes were known, however, the proliferation score was found not to recover the mitotic cell-cycle programme there (ρ = 0.03, *P* = 0.38), so — a post hoc judgement that leaves the locked verdict unchanged — muscle cannot test the coupling in either direction and is not used as evidence (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Tables 9 and 10).

Of the six preregistered hypotheses four met their criterion — H3 among them, for the reason just given, so that verdict is reported and not interpreted — one was not met and one could not be evaluated. Collagen formation in lower-leg skin rose with age (+0.027 per decade, *P* = 0.018) instead of falling (H6), and because GTEx cultures had no unadjusted age effect on the splicing set (*P* = 0.33) the preregistered test of attenuation could not be evaluated (H4). Separately, in an exploratory analysis, the collagen age association seen in the fibroblast cohort did not appear in GTEx cultures (+0.017 per decade, FDR = 0.33). Under the preregistered rule, age effects in the GTEx tissues are not used to support any claim.

Two alternatives to a biological reading were examined post hoc (Supplementary Fig. S8a, b). Proliferation varies less between tissue donors than between cultures, which would weaken a correlation even if the underlying relationship were unchanged; but the regression slope, which range restriction does not affect, falls with it (0.55 in culture, 0.17 in leg skin, 0.07 in suprapubic skin), the value predicted for skin by a range-restriction correction is 0.65 against the 0.23 observed, and cultures subsampled to the skin's proliferation spread still give partial ρ ≈ 0.77 to 0.87. Cell composition accounts for about half of what remains in sun-exposed skin and for all of the small, non-significant coupling in non-exposed skin, and the adjustment does not remove the proliferation signal itself. Adding cell-composition marker scores to the covariates (keratinocyte, fibroblast and immune) lowers the splicing coupling from 0.23 to 0.10 in sun-exposed skin and from 0.06 to −0.06 in non-exposed skin, while the culture splicing coupling is unaffected (0.74 to 0.72; the muscle values are in Supplementary Table 10). The same adjustment leaves the mitotic cell-cycle programme where it was (0.670 to 0.666 in sun-exposed skin, 0.597 to 0.586 in non-exposed skin) and leaves collagen formation where it was, and the two Reactome splicing definitions that were not selected in these data fall with the rest (Fig. 6d). The adjustment therefore leaves the cell-cycle programme's coupling intact while halving the splicing coupling. It was applied to six sets, not to every programme, and because the fibroblast marker score contains *COL1A1* and *COL1A2*, collagen formation is not an independent check of it. This adjustment extends a preregistered exploratory analysis, which applied composition markers to age effects, and is itself post hoc (Supplementary Note 1). Nor does the state of a donor's cultured fibroblasts track that donor's skin: across 388 donors who gave both, no programme score was related between the two (a preregistered exploratory analysis; ρ = −0.10 to 0.13, none with FDR < 0.05), although genotype-driven genes, added post hoc as a positive control, were recovered in the same samples (*GSTM1* and *ERAP2*, ρ = 0.75; Supplementary Fig. S8c, Supplementary Results 8).

What remains of the age association after adjustment is not secure, and the second cohort does not replicate it; this comparison was not preregistered. In the 107-donor cohort, which also contributed to defining the splicing set, proliferation fell with donor age (ρ = −0.46); the unadjusted age effect on the 96-gene set was −0.128 per decade (*P* = 4 × 10⁻⁶) and adjustment left −0.033 (95% CI −0.064 to −0.002, *P* = 0.04). In GTEx cultures, which played no part in defining the set, proliferation rose slightly with age (ρ = +0.10), the unadjusted age effect was absent (−0.025, *P* = 0.33), and adjustment revealed a decline of −0.053 (−0.087 to −0.019, *P* = 0.002; Fig. 6c). The unadjusted age association of splicing factors in culture therefore depends on how proliferation happens to move with age in a given collection. The GTEx estimate, however, is a suppression effect and we do not read it as a replication: the adjustment did not shrink an age effect but created one where none was measurable, and it does so for most of what it is applied to. Of 25 ageing programmes scored in these cultures, 5 have an unadjusted age effect at *P* < 0.05 and 15 acquire a negative adjusted one, the mitotic cell-cycle positive control among them (+0.006, *P* = 0.75 unadjusted; −0.021, *P* = 0.002 adjusted) — an age-related decline of the cell-cycle programme independent of proliferation is not a biological result, and the preregistration had already set that comparison aside as uninterpretable (Supplementary Note 1). The donor-cohort estimate is specification-dependent in its own right: −0.040 with the cohort covariates, −0.022 (*P* = 0.11) with sequencing depth alone, and −0.016 to −0.022 once any second gene-expression programme — translation, rRNA processing or Metabolism of RNA — enters the model beside proliferation (Supplementary Table 5). We therefore report no age effect on splicing-factor expression that is independent of proliferative state.

# Discussion

Splicing-factor transcripts fall as human fibroblasts age in culture and as donor age rises, and the genes that fall most are those the ageing literature names most often. The results here place that observation: these genes are transcribed in step with proliferative state — with a division rate that was measured directly rather than inferred, and more closely still with the transcriptional growth programme — and the interventions that appear to restore them are interventions that make cells divide. A comparison with tissue from the GTEx donor pool marks where that reading stops: between donors, the splicing genes track proliferation strongly in cultured fibroblasts and weakly in skin, where the same score still recovers the replication programmes and, in a post hoc adjustment, about half of what is left is accounted for by cell-composition markers. That the machinery follows growth is expected from the way it is regulated^10^; what is added here is the size of the effect in the systems where the ageing claim is made, the boundary at which it stops, and the finding that no residual independent of proliferation could be secured (Fig. 7). The same question has been answered for DNA methylation, where the age-associated loss of methylation in late-replicating domains tracks cumulative population doublings and slows in proportion when the cell cycle is slowed^25^, and where the methylation signatures of ageing run in the same direction in cultured fibroblasts but about sixty times faster^26^. A preregistered test of whether methylation clocks computed in the cultures used here follow the same variable met one of its four criteria (Supplementary Note 2), and no statement about clocks is made here.

The six results draw on datasets that overlap, and the overlaps are stated where they occur: the counted resource contributes to Figs 1, 2, 4 and 5, the donor cohort to Figs 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6c, and the donor cohort and GTEx were both reprocessed by recount3 (Methods). What links the figures is two instruments and a chain of expectations. The 177-gene set was defined in Fig. 1 and not altered afterwards; its earlier 96-gene form, fixed by the preregistration, is used in GTEx (Methods). The 20-marker proliferation score was checked against cell counting in the one resource that has counts (ρ = 0.63; Fig. 2e) and then applied wherever counts are missing. Each dataset tests an expectation the previous one raises: if the set falls with age because cells divide more slowly, it should follow the counted rate (Fig. 2); if it follows the rate, proliferation should account for most of the age effect (Fig. 3); if it reports proliferative state, whatever the source of the change, any perturbation of division should move it (Fig. 4), other growth-coupled programmes should behave alike (Fig. 5), and the coupling should weaken where cells do not divide (Fig. 6). The five culture values collected in Fig. 7b lie between ρ = 0.63 and 0.91, and the two skin values fall to 0.23 and 0.06; finer units within the counted resource give lower values (0.54 across 41 series, 0.49 across treatment-matched differences, 0.32 in the weakest cell line). The counted resource and the donor cohort each enter more than one figure and both contributed to defining the set, so the culture results are not independent of one another; the GTEx cultures, which played no part in defining the set, give the same coupling, and no single batch, cohort or pipeline is common to all of these datasets. What they share is a growth-coupled programme.

Whether anything survives adjustment cannot be settled here. The set still declines by about four hundredths of a standard deviation per decade in adult donors, but that figure halves again when a second gene-expression programme is put in the model beside proliferation, and the second collection of cultured fibroblasts does not replicate it: there the adjustment creates an age effect rather than shrinking one, and creates one for most programmes it is applied to. Which individual genes carry the residue in the first cohort changes with the cohort definition, and no gene keeps a decline under all four definitions examined here, one of which keeps none. A residual, if there is one, is a property of the set rather than a usable marker panel. The attenuation itself should not be over-read either: in a cohort where proliferation falls with age, the same adjustment removes a similar share of the age effect from almost any gene set that has one. What is specific here is the magnitude of the association, before and after adjustment, which no expression-matched random set approached.

This does not weaken the case for splicing in ageing; it separates two measurements that are often used interchangeably. The evidence that splicing outcome deteriorates with age is built on the product of splicing measured in tissues^8^; what this study measures is what the abundance of the machinery reports in culture, and the two are not interchangeable. In these cells an outcome index computed from the same libraries carries no age association in the adult donor cohort (Supplementary Results 3), and the association seen when the deposited cohort is used as a whole is traceable to progeria donors, children, and an oldest stratum drawn entirely from one cell repository — so this cohort cannot test the tissue claim either way. None of this argues against a functional role for the spliceosome in senescence, since inhibiting spliceosomal genes is by itself enough to drive fibroblasts into senescence^12^; the question here is what the expression measurement reports when it is used as a readout of ageing.

Extending the same test to the recognised hallmarks shows how far the problem reaches. Eight of twenty-three ageing programmes track measured division rate at |ρ| > 0.5, four of them as tightly as the cell-cycle gene set, and they are the programmes whose products are consumed in proportion to nascent transcription and DNA replication: spliceosome, chromatin, replication-coupled repair, telomere maintenance. For four of them the coupling persists after every cell-cycle gene is removed, so it is not an artefact of set overlap. Reading any of them in a cultured fibroblast as a measure of biological age carries the same risk as reading splicing factors that way. Cancer transcriptomics met the same problem earlier: most published prognostic signatures were found to report proliferation, and removing cell-cycle genes from a signature did not remove the dependence^27^, which is why the splicing and cell-cycle sets are also compared here with their shared genes excluded (ρ = 0.85 against 0.86; Results). The curated senescence set is the sharpest case, because it is used as an endpoint in intervention work^28^ and, in this material, its aggregate score reports the opposite of its own markers: faster-dividing cultures score higher, while *CDKN1A*, *CDKN2A*, *CDKN2B* and *ATM* behave as expected. An aggregate pathway score is only as specific as its least specific members, and curated senescence sets are built to be inclusive: the Reactome set, 82 of whose 150 measured genes are cell-cycle genes, scores higher in faster-dividing cultures, whereas SenMayo, with one such gene among the 88 measured here, does not follow division rate. A senescence score computed in cultured cells should therefore be reported with the panel named and its cell-cycle content stated (Supplementary Fig. S6).

The comparison with tissue changes what the culture result means. The same splicing genes that track proliferation strongly in cultured fibroblasts barely track it in skin, where the same score still recovers the replication programmes. A donor's cultured fibroblasts show no detectable concordance with the programme-level state of that donor's skin even though, in a positive control added post hoc, genotype-driven expression differences survive culture in the same samples. Culture imposes one dominant axis of variation, and it is the axis that also changes with passage, confluence and most interventions. An ageing signature defined in culture for any of the coupled programmes is therefore a statement about proliferative state until it has been shown in the tissue of interest. What is shown here is one tissue, sampled at two sites. Skeletal muscle, where the proliferation score does not recover the mitotic cell-cycle programme, could not test the coupling in either direction, so the reported changes in spliceosomal proteins in aged human muscle^29^ and the age-associated splicing changes mapped across human tissues^30^ are neither supported nor questioned by these data. Age-related expression changes in human tissues have themselves been linked to a decline in proliferation^31^; the comparison here is between donors within a tissue, where proliferation varies little, and there the splicing set tracks it weakly in skin.

Two features make the coupling difficult to avoid in cultured fibroblasts. It is strong rather than marginal, leaving little variance in which a splicing-specific signal could sit. And the experimental systems in which splicing factors are most often measured — replicative senescence, passage series, reprogramming, growth-promoting media — are systems in which division rate is the main variable that changes.

What follows is a matter of practice. A division-rate measurement is inexpensive and is already collected in most passage experiments, and reporting it beside a splicing-factor result allows a reader to judge the claim. Where a cohort is used to estimate an age effect, the direction in which proliferation moves with age in that cohort should be reported, because it decides whether the raw effect is inflated or hidden; and the composition of a fibroblast panel deserves the same scrutiny, since in the collection used here the oldest donors and one cell repository are the same donors, and disease lines are deposited alongside normal ones.

The limits are clear. Tissue was examined only in skin and skeletal muscle, at the level of programme expression; splicing outcome was not measured in tissue, and GTEx provides donor age only in ten-year bands. Age effects measured within the GTEx tissues did not pass the preregistered positive control and are not interpreted; the tissue comparison itself, which is about how strongly the splicing genes track proliferation in each tissue, does not depend on them. Division rate was counted in one resource; elsewhere, including GTEx, a transcriptional proliferation score stands in for it, and the two do not agree for every programme: against the proliferation score in GTEx cultures the unfolded protein response reaches 0.70 (0.05 against the counted rate), the TCA cycle 0.74 (0.29) and translation 0.75 (0.43), while chromatin organisation falls to 0.43 (0.69; Supplementary Tables 6 and 10). Contrasts were assembled from data deposited by others, with the heterogeneity that entails. One donor-age cohort was excluded because its processing batches coincide almost exactly with age strata; it enters only the definition of the preregistered 96-gene set used in GTEx, where, post hoc, the 177-gene set gives the same verdicts. Two of the three series that define the splicing set also provide sample-level data on donor age and culture time, so the age associations shown there are descriptive; redefining the set with the donor-age series recomputed in the primary cohort changes 3 of 179 genes and no conclusion, and the coupling to proliferation is replicated in GTEx, which played no part in defining the set. Division rate was not manipulated by us. Nine treatment arms of the counted resource do manipulate it, and the splicing score then follows the transcriptional proliferation programme more closely than the counted rate: where pharmacology or oxygen tension separates the two, five of the nine arms keep a residual effect with the counted rate held constant, and oligomycin and growth at 3% oxygen keep one that neither measure explains (Supplementary Results 1). That is the same disagreement between the two measures of proliferation that appears in GTEx. Finally, bulk tissue consists mostly of cells that are not cycling, and a proliferation score compared between donors in tissue largely tracks the fraction of cycling cells, so the coupling could be absent between donors in bulk tissue while still holding within each dividing cell; the tissue result is a statement about what the measurement reports, not about regulation within a cell.

The question came from an intervention experiment. Conditioned medium collected during partial reprogramming improves the phenotype of ultraviolet-damaged human dermal fibroblasts and extends lifespan in *Caenorhabditis elegans* (Shim, V. *et al.*, manuscript submitted), and splicing factors are among the programmes in which such an effect would be read. That work is cited here as the earlier study that prompted the question; no data are shared with it and none of its findings are tested. What the present analysis supplies is the reading the measurement carries on its own: in a cultured fibroblast, a rise in splicing-factor transcripts after an intervention is first of all evidence of a change in proliferative state rather than of a splicing-specific effect, and whether the cells are in fact dividing faster needs a division rate measured beside the transcriptome, since in the counted resource dexamethasone raises the splicing score without raising the counted rate (Supplementary Results 1, Supplementary Table 2).

# Methods

## Datasets and contrasts

Sixty-three contrasts in human fibroblasts were assembled from 23 GEO series (Supplementary Table 4). Where count matrices were deposited, counts were TMM-normalised^32,33^ and a contrast computed as the difference in mean log₂(CPM + 0.5) between arms, retaining genes with CPM ≥ 1 in at least two samples; where only a differential-expression table was deposited, the published log₂ fold change was used. Identifiers were mapped to HGNC symbols and sample-to-arm assignments verified against series metadata. Classes were assigned by experimental manipulation; two classifications were corrected after inspecting the deposited protocols, and are listed in Supplementary Table 4. For the two intervention classes, class residuals were also computed post hoc from fits that exclude the class being predicted (Supplementary Results 5). A contrast's score for a gene set is the mean log₂ fold change of the set's genes minus the mean over all measured genes; the 63 contrasts were correlated by Spearman's ρ, refitted without the three series that also define the splicing set, and compared with 1,000 gene sets matched to the splicing set for expression level and containing no splicing or cell-cycle genes; class residuals from the 63-contrast regression were tested against zero with a one-sample *t* test and corrected by Benjamini–Hochberg.

## Donor cohort

The donor-age cohort is GSE113957^15^, obtained as uniformly reprocessed gene and junction sums^17^. Sample attributes deposited with the runs give each donor's age, sex, disease status and cell identifier. Ten donors annotated with Hutchinson–Gilford progeria were excluded, because the disease is caused by activation of a cryptic *LMNA* splice donor^16^ and the cells proliferate poorly; 26 normal donors under 20 years of age were excluded, because the question is adult ageing. The primary cohort is the remaining 107 normal donors aged 20 to 96 years. Cell identifiers show that all 31 donors aged 83 and over come from one repository (Coriell AG series) while most donors under 20 come from another, so the repository, the sequencing instrument and sex enter every donor-level model as covariates, together with log₁₀ sequencing depth. Results under three further definitions — all 133 normal donors, normal adults aged 20 to 82, and the deposited 142-sample set — are given in Supplementary Table 1. The deposited set has 142 samples rather than 143 because one progeria library (SRR7093948) has no junction metrics and was not carried into the analysis tables; it therefore contains nine progeria donors. Expression was filtered, TMM-normalised and standardised within each cohort separately.

## Gene sets

The splicing set was defined from three series of cultured dermal fibroblasts: donor age in GSE113957 and GSE307377, and late versus early passages in GSE179848. A gene was included if it was measured in all three series, fell in every one, and belongs to Reactome^34^ mRNA splicing, capped intron-containing pre-mRNA processing or 3′-end processing (177 genes). A version defined before a fourth cohort was excluded, which also required agreement in that cohort, has 96 genes, all contained in the 177; the GTEx preregistration fixed that version. That cohort, GSE226189, was excluded from the study because its processing batches coincide with age strata (Kruskal–Wallis test of donor age across batches, *P* < 0.001); it enters only the definition of the preregistered 96-gene set. Redefining the 177-gene set with the GSE113957 contrast recomputed in the primary cohort gives 179 genes, 176 of them shared. Enrichment was assessed by hypergeometric test against Reactome sets of 15 to 500 genes, within the genes measured in all three series, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction^35^. The cell-cycle set is the union of Reactome Cell Cycle, Mitotic, DNA Replication and M Phase (533 genes, 23 shared with the splicing set). The proliferation score is the mean gene-wise *z*-score of 20 proliferation markers (*MKI67*, *CCNB1*, *CCNA2*, *CDK1*, *TOP2A*, *BIRC5*, *BUB1*, *PLK1*, *AURKA*, *TYMS*, *RRM2*, *PCNA*, *MCM2*–*MCM7*, *TK1*, *UBE2C*).

## Division rate

Division rate is taken from the Cellular Lifespan Study^13,14^, which records the population doubling time of each culture in hours per division from cell counts at passage; it is expressed here as divisions per day. Because the 328 libraries are repeated measures of seven cell lines, the pooled correlation is reported with the within-line correlations and with linear mixed models fitted with the cell line as a random intercept, with a random slope, and with days in culture added as a fixed effect. Aggregating to one value per cell line, treatment and oxygen tension gives 41 series.

## Treatment perturbation

Nine treatment arms of the counted resource^13,14^ were used to test the coupling under perturbation: contact inhibition, dexamethasone, oligomycin, mitoNUITs (a combination of UK5099, etomoxir and BPTES), their two combinations with dexamethasone, 2-deoxyglucose, β-hydroxybutyrate and galactose. The splicing score is the 177-gene score of Fig. 2. Arms with fewer than five libraries or present in only one cell line were to be left out of the models; none was (Supplementary Table 2a). For the descriptive comparison, each treated culture was matched to the control culture of the same cell line and oxygen tension nearest in days grown, and the difference in division rate, splicing score and proliferation score was taken (224 of 224 treated cultures matched; median difference in days grown 0). For the models, the splicing score was regressed on treatment with cell line, oxygen tension and days grown as covariates, and then with either the measured division rate or the transcriptional proliferation score added; a treatment is counted as keeping a residual effect when its coefficient is significant at BH-FDR < 0.05 within the model, and as accounted for by the adjustment otherwise. The same models were fitted with the proliferation score as the response. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied within each model. Because that comparison holds oxygen tension fixed, the effect of 3% against 21% oxygen was examined separately among control cultures, matched in the same way and modelled with cell line and days grown as covariates. These analyses were designed after the main results were known and are reported as post hoc.

## Programme audit

Twenty-three Reactome pathways standing for recognised hallmarks of ageing^20,21^, together with *Cell Cycle, Mitotic* as a positive control, were taken from the Reactome pathway file (sets of at least twenty genes). Each was scored per library as the mean of the gene-wise *z*-scores of log₂ counts per million, in the 328 counted libraries for division rate and in the 107 adult donors for donor age, restricting each set to the genes expressed in that matrix. Correlations are Spearman; donor-age *P* values come from linear models with the cohort covariates; Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied separately within the division-rate family and the donor-age family. Each programme was scored a second time with every gene belonging to the cell-cycle union removed. The Cellular Senescence set was dissected by intersecting its members with Reactome *Cell Cycle* and *Cell Cycle, Mitotic* and comparing the two resulting groups of gene-wise correlations with a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The SenMayo panel^22^ was taken from MSigDB and scored identically. The full table is Supplementary Table 6.

## Senescence panels

Five further panels were scored identically to the programmes above for the comparison in Supplementary Fig. S6: SenMayo^22^ and the two halves of the Fridman and Tainsky signature^36^ from MSigDB, and the CellAge build 3 genes^37^ split by their annotated effect on senescence. A panel's cell-cycle content is the share of its measured genes belonging to the union of Reactome cell-cycle pathways, the same union used in Fig. 5a. This comparison is post hoc.

## Methylation clocks

DNA methylation arrays from the same cell lines and passages^13^ (GSE179847) were used to compute four published clocks — Horvath's multi-tissue clock^23^, the skin and blood clock^38^, PhenoAge^39^ and Hannum's clock^40^ — from the deposited beta matrix restricted to the clock CpGs (334 of 353, 391 of 391, 513 of 513 and 63 of 69 available). Measurements with a detection *P* above 0.01 were set to missing (0.018% of measurements), no sample was missing more than 30% of clock CpGs, and the 99 remaining gaps were filled with the cohort mean of that CpG. Because this reprocessing does not reproduce the original normalisation pipeline, absolute ages are approximations; only ranks and residuals are interpreted. Clock acceleration is the residual of DNAm age on days in culture within a cell line. Methylation samples were matched to RNA libraries by the study's own culture identifier, giving 471 methylation samples with a measured division rate from nine cell lines and nineteen treatments, and 109 cultures carrying both measurements. The four hypotheses, their success criteria, the exclusion rules and the limits on reporting were fixed in a preregistration whose SHA-256 digest was recorded before the methylation matrix was downloaded (Supplementary Note 2); the analysis script verifies that digest before it runs. All four outcomes are reported in Supplementary Table 8.

## Culture and tissue in GTEx

Gene sums and sample metadata for the GTEx v8^24^ skin and skeletal-muscle projects were obtained from recount3^17^; only processed files were used, and base-coverage sums were treated as counts as for the donor cohort. Samples annotated as cultured fibroblasts, sun-exposed lower-leg skin, non-exposed suprapubic skin and skeletal muscle were retained, samples in the lowest 1% of library size within a tissue were excluded, and one sample per donor per tissue was kept. Each tissue was normalised separately (filtering by expression, TMM, log₂ CPM, gene-wise *z*-scores). Programmes were scored as in the programme audit after the twenty proliferation markers had been removed from every programme, so that no programme shares genes with the proliferation score. The splicing set in these analyses is the preregistered 96-gene version. Partial Spearman correlations were computed after regressing RNA integrity number, ischaemic time and log₁₀ library size out of both variables; tissues were compared with Fisher's *z* using a variance of 1.06/(*n* − 3 − *k*). Age, released as ten-year bands, entered linear models as band midpoints together with sex, Hardy death classification and the technical covariates, with and without the proliferation score. Hypotheses, definitions, exclusions and success criteria were preregistered, and the document was locked by its SHA-256 digest before the data were downloaded; the analysis script checks the digest before it runs. The preregistration was an internal record, not a public registration, and its English translation is Supplementary Note 1. The six primary hypotheses were corrected together by Benjamini–Hochberg, and programme-level analyses within each tissue. Analyses added after the outcomes were known are labelled post hoc where they are reported and are recorded in Supplementary Note 1: the identification of 32 off-trend cultures, the repetition with the 177-gene set, the regression slopes, the range-restriction prediction (Thorndike's case II, from the ratio of the proliferation-score standard deviations in skin and culture) and variance-matched subsampling, the adjustment for cell-composition marker scores and its repetition for the cell-cycle positive control, collagen formation and the two unselected splicing definitions rather than the splicing sets alone, the genotype-driven positive control for donor concordance, the comparison of machinery sets in GTEx cultures and the refitting of the donor-cohort residual with comparator machinery scores; the comparison donor cohort was also redefined after the outcomes were known, which affects every donor-level analysis in this paper and not only Fig. 6c.

## Splicing outcome and event-level analysis

Junction counts for the donor cohort were taken from recount3^17^. The outcome index is the fraction of junction-spanning reads assigned to junctions absent from the reference annotation; the fraction of distinct unannotated junctions and the mean Shannon entropy of junction usage within 5′ splice-site groups with at least 20 reads are reported alongside. Sequencing depth was a covariate in every model. This index was not specified in advance of the analyses in which it first appeared, and is reported here as an exploratory measure. For event-level analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3c, d), junctions with at least five reads in at least half the donors were retained and grouped by shared donor site (alternative acceptors) or shared acceptor site (alternative donors). Percent spliced in is a junction's reads as a fraction of its group total; events were kept where the group total reached 20 reads in at least 80% of donors. Logit-transformed values were tested against age with and without proliferation using moderated *t*-statistics^41^ and Benjamini–Hochberg correction^35^, under each cohort definition.

## Age predictor

A ridge regression was trained on the log₂ counts per million of all genes passing the expression filter in the 107 donors, with genes standardised and the 5,000 most variable retained inside each training fold, and the penalty chosen by an inner five-fold cross-validation on the training fold only. Accuracy is from ten-fold cross-validation repeated five times, and the null is twenty repetitions with permuted ages. Two further models were fitted the same way: one on expression from which the cohort covariates had been regressed out, and one from which the proliferation score had also been regressed out, both fitted within the training folds. A model using the proliferation score as the only predictor was cross-validated identically. This analysis is post hoc.

## Sensitivity analyses

Four checks were run on the donor cohort and are reported in Supplementary Table 3. Technical quality measures from the reprocessing pipeline — uniquely mapped percentage, multi-mapping percentage, intronic signal and exon assignment rate — were added to the donor models as covariates. The splicing set was split into the 127 genes belonging to Reactome mRNA splicing and the 50 genes of capping, 3′-end processing and export, and each was modelled separately. As a negative control for the proliferation adjustment, 1,000 gene sets matched to the splicing set for expression level, and containing no splicing or cell-cycle genes, were drawn and put through the same unadjusted and adjusted models. Finally, because the reprocessed gene sums are base-coverage sums rather than read counts and the libraries were sequenced at two read lengths (51 and 75 nucleotides), all scores were recomputed after dividing coverage by read length; the scores agree with the coverage-based ones (*r* = 0.99) and every estimate is unchanged. To find which technical term the residual age effect depends on, the two donor models were also refitted with sequencing depth as the only covariate and with depth plus the repository, the instrument or sex in turn (Supplementary Table 3).

## Machinery specificity and the residual age effect in two cohorts

To ask whether the coupling is specific to splicing, the splicing set was compared with the Reactome sets Translation, Metabolism of RNA and rRNA processing, scored identically as the mean gene-wise z. Because the 177-gene set was selected in these data, the comparison was repeated with the unselected Reactome sets mRNA Splicing and Processing of Capped Intron-Containing Pre-mRNA. Shared genes were removed from both sides for the unselected definitions and from the comparator for the selected set (removing them from both sides for the selected set as well changes no partial correlation by more than 0.01), except that Metabolism of RNA contains both splicing pathways, from which the splicing genes were removed only. All sets, the 177-gene set included, were scored in the expression matrix of the programme audit, in which 176 of the 177 genes pass the filter and the 177-gene score correlates with the counted rate at ρ = 0.68 (0.71 for the score of Fig. 2a). Partial Spearman correlations were computed on ranks, against the measured division rate in the counted libraries and against the 20-marker proliferation score in GTEx cultures (Supplementary Table 5). In the donor cohort the age models of the main analysis were refitted with the comparator score added alongside the proliferation score, in both directions. The residual age effects of the two fibroblast cohorts are reported side by side and are not pooled: with two studies the between-study variance is not estimable, and the two estimates are not the same quantity, one being an attenuation and the other a suppression effect. The donor cohort's residual was also refitted with each of three comparator machinery scores added beside the proliferation score, and with sequencing depth as the only technical covariate, and the range is reported (Supplementary Table 5). The four definitions of the fibroblast cohort, which are not independent of one another, are reported as sensitivity. Both analyses are post hoc.

## Statistics and reproducibility

Spearman correlations are used for monotone associations and Pearson where a linear fit is shown; correlations are reported with 95% confidence intervals from Fisher's *z*. *P* values are two-sided except for the preregistered one-sided comparison of the skin and culture correlations (H2). For correlations with fewer than ten observations the exact permutation distribution of Spearman's statistic is used rather than the normal approximation. On figure panels, *P* values below 2 × 10⁻¹⁶ are printed as *P* < 2 × 10⁻¹⁶ (Fig. 2a prints them in full); where the text reports the same test, it gives the exact value. Proliferation adjustment enters as a covariate at sample level and as the residual from a regression of the splicing-set change on the cell-cycle change at contrast level. Every *P* value quantifies an association computed over genes, samples, donors or contrasts as stated. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size; no randomisation or blinding applies to analyses of deposited data. The expectations that open Results sections 2 to 6 are the authors' reasoning, formulated in the order the analyses were run; only the GTEx hypotheses (Supplementary Note 1) and the methylation hypotheses (Supplementary Note 2) were fixed before the data were seen. Fig. 7b collects correlations already reported, each with the unit and comparator of its own analysis.
